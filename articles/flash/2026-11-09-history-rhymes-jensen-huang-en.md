# Oppenheimer's Script, Now Starring Jensen Huang?

History Rhymes, Special Edition · November 9, 2026 · Guangyi Zhao

On November 9, Nvidia's stock fell 5.27% intraday, closing near $176.81. It was not an earnings blowup. It was not that the chips stopped selling. It was because, the day before, its board of directors had announced that it was investigating its own CEO.

A board publicly investigating the company's founder is rare in the history of U.S. stocks. Rarer still is the stated reason: whether Jensen Huang had become the target of a foreign intelligence operation. In this special edition of History Rhymes, we place this episode on a historical line stretching back more than seventy years: How many times has this script been performed? And how will this performance end?

## I. First, Separate Fact from Allegation

Let us start with what has been confirmed, then turn to what has merely been reported. That line must not be blurred. Blurring it is how rumors are made.

What has been confirmed: On Sunday, November 8, Nvidia's board announced an independent investigation, overseen by a committee of independent directors and assisted by outside counsel. The board's statement was restrained: it said there was currently no reason to believe that U.S. national security or company secrets had been harmed, that it retained confidence in Huang and his team, and that the investigation had no set timetable. That same day, Huang wrote a letter to all employees (the Financial Times obtained its contents): he admitted that he had been unfaithful to his wife, apologized to his family, and pledged to cooperate fully with the investigation. The extramarital affair is something he himself has admitted. On that point, the record is settled.

What has only been reported, and remains unsubstantiated: The matter originated with an October 29 report in The New York Times. According to that report, U.S. intelligence officials concluded that Alice Huang (whose Chinese name is Huang Xiaole, and who is not related to Jensen Huang), an executive at the Singapore data center company Megaspeed, was suspected of passing information to Beijing, and that surveillance showed a close relationship between her and Jensen Huang; but the intelligence officials' initial assessment, according to the report, was that Huang himself was unaware of any such activity. Megaspeed is a Singapore company spun off from a Chinese company, and according to reports, its Malaysian affiliate purchased at least $4.8 billion worth of AI equipment containing Nvidia chips between June 2024 and February 2026. On November 4, the chairs of the House Foreign Affairs Committee, the Select Committee on the Chinese Communist Party, and the House Intelligence Committee jointly wrote to Huang, asking him to attend a voluntary closed-door briefing on November 18 and to reply by November 11.

As of today: no one has been charged, and there is no hard evidence to support the espionage allegations. Singapore's Ministry of Home Affairs has publicly questioned parts of the reporting, while China's Ministry of Foreign Affairs has denied the allegations and called them a smear. In other words, the person under suspicion, according to these reports, is Alice Huang; Jensen Huang's role in the existing reporting is that of "the person who was targeted," not "the person against whom anything has been proven." In the Chinese-language public sphere, that distinction has already been lost.

## II. This Script Was Already Performed in 1954

The closest parallel is Oppenheimer.

In 1954, J. Robert Oppenheimer, the father of the atomic bomb, was dragged into a security clearance hearing. What the hearing dug up will look familiar today: not evidence of treason, but his extramarital affair, his early left-wing associations, and his close dealings with people who were themselves under suspicion. The final conclusion was that no evidence had been found that he was disloyal to the country — but his security clearance was revoked all the same, and he was out. That revocation was not formally overturned by the U.S. Department of Energy until 2022 — sixty-eight years too late, and fifty-five years after his death.

The method is the same: when a person is too important to be moved through ordinary procedures, you use his private life and his circle of associations to send him into a security review process. The process itself does not need to convict. The process itself is the punishment.

There are three more story lines, all of them involving Chinese faces. In 1950, Qian Xuesen was accused and placed under house arrest; in 1955 he was deported back to China, where he went on to build China's missile and space programs — something Americans themselves later described as the stupidest thing done that year. In 1999, Wen Ho Lee of Los Alamos was accused of stealing nuclear secrets and jailed for nine months, until the case against him collapsed as a whole and a federal judge apologized to him in open court. The most recent line is from 2025: Intel's Chinese American CEO, Lip-Bu Tan, was publicly demanded by Trump to resign because of his China-related investments; in the end he stayed, at the price of the U.S. government taking a stake of roughly ten percent in Intel.

Line the four stories up side by side, and the pattern is clear: the more a company sits at the throat of a strategic industry, and the more deeply the founder as an individual is bound up with the company, the more easily something like this is triggered. The position Nvidia occupies today is the position Los Alamos occupied then: a nation's most critical technology, held in the hands of one specific person.

## III. Two Popular Explanations, Each Half Right

Since this story broke, two explanations have circulated most widely in Chinese-language circles. My view is that each gets half of it right, and each misses half.

The first: blame it on the fact that he never set up a dual-class share structure, never built himself an equity defense.

Huang holds about 3.5% of Nvidia's shares, and Nvidia has a one-share, one-vote structure. Compare Mark Zuckerberg: protected by a dual-class structure, he survived a storm as large as Cambridge Analytica without the Meta board even having a door through which to begin a removal process. The counterexample for one-share, one-vote is Steve Jobs — in 1985, he was driven out of the Apple he founded by the CEO he himself had brought in. So that half is correct: if this matter ultimately comes down to a board vote on whether Huang stays or goes, he has no structural defense in his hands. His only shield at the moment is the phrase "retains confidence" in the board's statement — and the board can take that phrase back at any time.

But the other half is this: the source of the pressure this time is not shareholders, and it is not a boardroom coup. It is the machinery of the state — the intelligence system, congressional committees, and export controls that could be tightened at any moment. Dual-class shares can block a shareholders' meeting. They cannot block a closed-door congressional briefing, still less a single export rule issued by the Department of Commerce. Both Uber's Travis Kalanick and WeWork's Adam Neumann held super-voting rights, and both were forced out anyway under scandal and pressure. An equity structure determines who can fire you. It does not determine whether the state investigates you.

The second explanation: this is the Anglo-Saxon system "picking the peaches" — it cannot tolerate a person of Chinese descent sitting on the throne of AI.

The historical basis this claim points to does genuinely exist. Qian Xuesen, Wen Ho Lee, and a group of scholars of Chinese descent in the "China Initiative" whose cases were ultimately dropped — the pattern of "intense suspicion first, no solid evidence in the end" has happened to this group again and again. That is a matter of record, not imagination.

But applied to this particular case, it fails in three places. First, the trigger was an extramarital affair, and that is a fact Huang himself has admitted, not something anyone fabricated against him. Second, the questions surrounding Megaspeed are a genuine law-enforcement issue: a company spun off from a Chinese firm, buying $4.8 billion of AI equipment through a Malaysian affiliate — against the backdrop of export controls, transactions like that are exactly the kind that sit on law enforcement's radar, and in recent years there have indeed been GPU smuggling cases that were prosecuted and resulted in convictions. Third, Wall Street's interests are tied to Huang's. He is the goose that lays the golden eggs; capital has no motive to pick him off. The pressure this time comes from the security establishment, not from shareholders. And let us not forget that the line of white founders brought down by scandal and investigation is just as long: Steve Wynn lost the company that bears his own name, and neither Les Moonves nor Kalanick is of Chinese descent.

So I do not accept a purely "peach-picking" narrative. But I do not accept a complete denial of the ethnic factor, either.

## IV. A More Accurate Model: Three Variables, Stacked

I prefer to explain events of this kind with three variables. Every historical case fits inside them:

First, the founder has no structural control. He holds few shares, the company has one share and one vote, or — as with Oppenheimer — he is merely an employee scientist. The decision over whether he stays or goes rests in other people's hands.

Second, the company or institution sits at a strategic chokepoint in a great-power competition. In 1954 it was nuclear weapons. Today it is AI computing power. A chokepoint position means that the state will carry out its scrutiny of a private individual as if it were exercising control over the technology itself.

Third, the person happens to have a real vulnerability. The vulnerability does not need to be a crime. An extramarital affair, private associations, early-life contacts — any one real, unsavory fact is enough to serve as the reason to start the process.

When all three variables are present, the process starts. In today's climate, an ethnic background will amplify the intensity and the speed of suspicion — the Qian Xuesen line is the evidence for that — but it is not the engine. The engine is the first three variables.

The reason is simple: this episode has put a market price on "Jensen Huang as a single point of failure," and the board will have to respond. Verification date: December 31, 2027.

## VI. The Landing Point

In closing, a word for ourselves.

If Huang gets through this, it will not be because of equity — he does not have it. It will be because of the fact itself that "Nvidia cannot do without him": the ability to make yourself indispensable is a more fundamental line of defense than any equity structure. But what this episode wants to remind us of is that this line of defense is not invincible either. In his day, Oppenheimer was likewise "the man they could not do without," and he was pushed out in the face of the process all the same. Ability determines how large a room you are invited into; the process determines whether you will be invited out of that room. Both deserve reverence.

And one more observation, left for the next episode: if, after the closed-door briefing on November 18, the topic slides from "whom Huang associated with" to "Nvidia's compliance regarding Southeast Asian data centers," then the nature of this matter will have changed — from a personal scandal to a regulatory event for an entire industry. At that point, today's drop of about 5% may turn out to have been only the prologue.
